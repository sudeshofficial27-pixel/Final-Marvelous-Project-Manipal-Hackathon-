How MedSignal turns stock counts into a warning

Every facility–medicine pair goes through the same five steps each night. Nothing is a black box: each number on the watchboard can be traced back to the line of arithmetic that produced it.

1. Forecast the demand, not just the average

Daily consumption is smoothed with an exponentially weighted average over the last 7 days, then a trend term fitted over 14 days is added so a rising outbreak isn't flattened away. The spread of the residuals gives σ, the day-to-day volatility — that is what carries uncertainty through the rest of the pipeline.

2. Project the shelf forward

Expected stock on day t is current stock, minus μ·t, plus any replenishment expected to land before day t. Deliveries are discounted by the supplier's on-time record rather than assumed to arrive.

3. Express risk as a probability

Cumulative demand over t days is approximately normal with mean μ·t and standard deviation σ·√t. The chance of running out by day t is therefore Φ((μ·t − available(t)) / (σ·√t)), taken as the worst day in the window. This is why the watchboard says "82% chance of stockout in 7 days" instead of a single date it can't actually promise.

4. Say how much to trust the number

Confidence drops when consumption is erratic (high σ/μ), when the stock count is stale, or when a facility has too little history. Low-confidence signals are still shown — they're just labelled, so a decision-maker knows when to ring the facility before acting on the figure.

5. Look past the single facility

A stockout redirects roughly 60% of that facility's demand onto its nearest neighbours within 40 km, split by travel distance. MedSignal feeds that extra load back into step 3 and re-runs it, three waves deep, which is how a single PHC running dry surfaces as a corridor-level warning days ahead.

Where the redistribution plan comes from

Deficits are sorted by urgency. For each one, donors are scored by surplus ÷ (1 + distance/25 km) so a slightly smaller stock next door beats a large one two hours away. Each transfer is rounded to pack size and its benefit is reported in days of cover gained at the receiving facility.

Running this on real data

The demo runs on a seeded simulation of the Chengalpattu network, shaped around three plausible events: a dengue-driven surge in the Tambaram corridor, snakebite season in the southern rural belt, and a delayed insulin consignment at the district warehouse. Swapping in live data means mapping four fields per facility — stock on hand, daily issues, pending orders, and coordinates. Nothing in the maths assumes simulated input.
